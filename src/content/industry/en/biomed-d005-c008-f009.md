# Referencing and Tracing Sources for Phase I Clinical Regulations

## Data Characteristics

Phase I clinical research regulations and Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) originate from regulatory documents published by official bodies like the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA), the World Health Organization (WHO), and the International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH). They also come from detailed operational guidelines developed internally by clinical trial institutions. These documents are typically in PDF, Word, or internal knowledge base formats.

Regulatory updates at the national level are relatively stable, usually revised every few years. Internal institutional SOPs may be updated several times a year due to project needs or external regulatory changes.

Regulatory documents typically have clear chapters, clause numbers, rigorous content, and dense technical terminology. They include many definitions, process descriptions, and responsibility assignments. SOP documents focus more on specific operational steps, form-filling specifications, quality control points, and emergency handling procedures. Common elements include "version number," "effective date," "revision record," "operating steps," and "observation indicators." Units often involve time (hours, days), dosage (mg, μg), and frequency (times/day).

## Constraints on "Referencing and Tracing Sources"

The official and rigorous nature of Phase I clinical regulatory documents requires source citations to be precise, down to specific sections or page numbers of the original text, to ensure compliance.

The frequency of regulatory updates necessitates regular incremental updates or version management for the knowledge base. This ensures that cited regulations are the latest effective versions, avoiding references to outdated provisions.

Documents contain specialized terminology and complex process descriptions. This requires a text chunking strategy that preserves semantic integrity, preventing splits that break key definitions or operational steps.

Tables and figures common in documents require careful attention to accuracy and readability when converted to searchable text. This ensures that their original meaning can be restored during citation.

The precision of measurement units requires accurate identification and presentation during citation. This avoids unit loss or confusion due to format conversion, which could affect an engineer's understanding of dosage or time requirements.

## Common Pitfalls

*   **Symptom**: The model's answer cites regulatory clauses that differ from the original text.
    **Reason**: The document chunking strategy is inappropriate, leading to semantically incomplete individual chunks. The model then infers based on incomplete information.
*   **Symptom**: When asked about a specific operational step, the system fails to cite the corresponding SOP document.
    **Reason**: The knowledge base is not updated in time, or document version management is disorganized. This leads to citations of outdated or non-current regulations.
*   **Symptom**: In context citations, table data or graphical information is missing or formatted incorrectly.
    **Reason**: The document parser has insufficient capability to handle complex structures (e.g., tables, embedded images). It fails to correctly extract and convert non-text content.

## Verification Steps

*   Ask questions about several key regulatory clauses or SOP steps. Verify that the model's cited content, including chapter, page number, and specific text, completely matches the original document.
*   Test relevant questions against recently updated regulations or SOP files. Confirm that the system can cite the latest version of the content.
*   Construct test questions containing complex tables or figures. Check the presentation and accuracy of this information in context citations to ensure readability.
