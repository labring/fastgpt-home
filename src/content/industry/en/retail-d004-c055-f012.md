# Franchise filing management: document chunking for document comparison

## What the documents look like

Franchise filing management handles legal disclosure and the commerce department system. It does not handle specific site engineering. The daily documents are franchise contracts, operations manuals, filing materials, and information disclosure documents. These materials share a set of traits: multiple parties, explicit periods, and dense fields. In one contract, the signing parties, the cooperation term, the fee clauses, and the territory often sit in different paragraphs. Document chunking must keep each retrieved passage traceable to its source text and preserve the subject, object, status, and valid scope needed for a judgement.

The materials update irregularly or by year. This page does not depend on update rhythm, so synchronization mechanisms are out of scope.

Two traits shape this task directly. First, the same contract carries several distinct roles and periods, so a chunk that loses the counterparty or the term cannot support comparison. Second, disclosure documents state conditions as of a named date, so a chunk that drops the date qualifier changes what the text supports.

## Constraints at the document chunking step

Segmentation completeness directly affects contract and transaction material comparison. If a conditional clause and the clause it constrains are split into two data blocks, a hit on one block cannot reconstruct the full meaning. Data blocks and retrieval indexes are distinct: one original data record can map to multiple indexes. The index is a retrieval entry point; the original data is the basis for checking. Retrieval itself is not authorization. Retrieved content serves as checking material only and cannot stand as proof that approval, signing, or filing is complete.

Hypothetical paper exercise

Source text for checking (fictional):

"Party A, XX Catering Management Co., Ltd., and Party B, XX Franchisee, signed the Franchise Contract in March 2024, with a term of three years, agreeing that Party B operates in XX District, XX City. In May 2024, Party A issued an Information Disclosure Document stating that as of April 30, 2024, Party A had two directly operated stores, each operating for at least one year."

Draft sentence to be judged: "The franchisor has met the two-stores-one-year condition, and the filing materials can be submitted."

The fields needed for judgement are the number of directly operated stores and their operating time. The source text gives only two directly operated stores as of April 30, 2024, each operating at least one year. It does not state any filing acceptance status. The draft sentence merges condition satisfaction with submittability, an unsupported statement.

## Configuration and implementation checks

| Check item | Hypothetical starting point for this exercise | Comparison object and reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Segmentation method | Split by contract clause and disclosure document section | Compare against the source heading hierarchy to confirm conditions and constraints are not separated |
| Citation cap | 2000 tokens as a hypothetical starting point | Compare against a single contract clause length to avoid truncating a whole clause |
| Minimum relevance | 0.3 as a hypothetical starting point | Compare against semantic retrieval or rerank scores; acts as a filter threshold only |
| Search mode | Full-text search for numbers and proper nouns | Compare against semantic retrieval results; number queries favor keyword hits |
| Index check | Distinguish original data from the indexes field | Compare against dataset.datas records to confirm one original record maps to multiple indexes |
| Post-update review | Check training and index status after material changes | Compare against upload records; a successful upload does not mean retrieval is updated |

Minimum relevance applies only in semantic retrieval or result reranking and does not carry permission or entity isolation. Libraries across different vector models cannot be associated together; verify identifiers first and exclude non-matching ones.

## Three common errors

1. Treating segmentation as a purely technical split and ignoring the binding between a conditional clause and the clause it constrains. The input is the original contract clause. The comparison is whether each resulting block can independently reference the subject and period. The observable result is whether the condition and its constraint land in the same block.

2. Treating a retrieval hit as authorization or completed filing. The input is a retrieved disclosure document fragment. The comparison is whether the source text states an acceptance or approval status. The observable result is whether the hit contains that status field.

3. Treating a successful upload as an updated index. The input is the material change record and training status. The comparison is the content actually retrievable after the change. The observable result is whether new content can be retrieved.

## How to verify the setup

1. Search the hypothetical contract text for "XX Catering Management Co., Ltd. XX Franchisee." Compare whether the returned block contains the signing parties, contract term, and operating territory together. The observable result is whether all three elements are readable in the same block.

2. Search the hypothetical disclosure document for "directly operated stores operating time." Compare whether the returned content preserves the qualifier "as of April 30, 2024." The observable result is whether the time range appears together with the store count.

3. Use an empty result to check downstream design. With a paper input of [], check how the system handles a no-hit case. The comparison is whether the downstream distinguishes an empty result from lack of authorization. The observable result is that the empty result is not interpreted as denial or conflict.

All of the above are paper checks. No retrieval, training, or filing submission was actually executed.
